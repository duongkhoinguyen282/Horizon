Simon Willison argues that attempting to rewrite legacy systems from scratch is rarely successful because the original system continues to accumulate technical debt while the new project struggles to keep pace with evolving business needs. This perspective challenges the common engineering impulse to abandon messy codebases, suggesting that incremental refactoring and migration strategies are more reliable for maintaining business continuity. The author notes that greenfield projects often fail because they lack full understanding of the original system's behavior, frequently resulting in two unmaintained systems running in production simultaneously.

## [Reflecting on the Infinite Capacity for Software Complexity](https://simonwillison.net/2026/Sep/6/zach-kehs/) ⭐️ 6.0/10

Zach Kehs observes that unlike physical buildings which have structural limits, software systems can accumulate technical debt and complexity indefinitely. He highlights that code can always become worse through additional layers of indirection or performance degradation. This perspective challenges software engineers to recognize that code quality is not naturally self-limiting. It serves as a reminder that without active maintenance, software systems can degrade far beyond the point of physical collapse. The reflection emphasizes that software lacks the physical constraints of gravity and material stress, allowing for endless layers of abstraction that can obscure logic and hinder performance. This highlights the unique nature of digital systems where 'collapse' is logical rather than structural.

**Background**: Technical debt refers to the future costs of rework or maintenance that arise from prioritizing speed over code quality. Layers of indirection are design choices that add complexity by manipulating values through references rather than direct access, which can make systems harder to debug over time.
